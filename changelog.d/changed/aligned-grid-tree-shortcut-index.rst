The shortcut index is a longitude/latitude-aligned grid tree rather than an H3 hexagon grid: 0.75° cells, each refining into a 4×4 block of 0.1875° cells where several zones meet. The cell is two integer divisions per level rather than a library call, so it vectorises over a batch, and the cells are exact in the coordinate frame the point-in-polygon kernel reads, so a zone whose polygons only reach across a cell edge is no longer a candidate inside it. Random ``timezone_at()`` lookups are faster, and ``timezone_ids_at()`` batches several times faster on uniformly random points; the published benchmark pages carry the figures. The index is larger on disk than H3's but holds about a third less in memory, since its arrays are the file, and ``TimezoneFinderL``'s heap shrinks with it. ``TimezoneFinder`` answers are essentially unchanged: 2,000,000 area-weighted random points answer exactly as before, and over all 4,113,237 distinct stored boundary vertices off the −180 meridian and the −90 parallel, whose reading changes separately, 19 answers move. Those 19 are the two cases where candidate order decides rather than geometry, and the order is per cell: 18 are points inside no polygon at all - slivers on the 7.5° meridians where the ocean zones meet, where ``timezone_at()`` returns the final candidate untested and never promised otherwise - and one is a point inside both ``Australia/Brisbane`` and ``Etc/GMT-9``, where the first containing candidate wins and both answers are equally truthful. ``TimezoneFinderL`` is an approximation *by cell*, so its answers move wherever a cell does. This is a binary format change: ``DATA_FORMAT_VERSION`` is 4 and the required ``timezonefinder-data`` floor moves with it.
