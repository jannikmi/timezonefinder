# Designing a candidate comparison

Comparing two *candidate* implementations of one stage - or one implementation across two environments - is a different measurement from the suites in [benchmarking and performance validation](benchmarking-and-performance-validation.md), which compare one implementation across commits or machines.

Three hand-rolled designs for it have produced wrong answers in this repository, all of them flattering the newer candidate. `docs/benchmarking_methodology.rst` carries those three failures and the numbers they cost.

- **Use `benchmarks/candidate_comparison.py`'s `compare_candidates` instead of writing the harness again.** Pass two named callables that each perform the *whole public call* for one input, never the stage in isolation; the harness alternates their order round by round, samples inputs at random, and reports `unresolved` when its two estimators disagree rather than the one that shows an effect.
- **Sweeping a parameter is a ladder of paired comparisons, and three of its failure modes are not obvious.** `scripts/measure_batch_break_even.py` is the worked example; `docs/benchmarking_methodology.rst` carries the reasoning. (1) **Hold the work per round constant across rungs** and normalise by what a round actually answered, not by the nominal target — a fixed call count per round puts the smallest and largest rungs in different measurement regimes and plots them as one curve. (2) **Draw the samples within one batch without replacement**: a repeated coordinate is answered from the batch's own cell lookup, and the saving grows with the batch size, so it fakes exactly the amortisation curve being measured. Cap the ladder at half the pool for the same reason — at a batch size equal to the pool there is one possible batch and drawing it every round is not sampling. (3) **`compare_candidates` charges a fixed cost per timed item** (a callable dispatch and the answer list beside the lookups), measured here at ~0.21 µs, and a per-point normalisation spreads it as `h/N`: 16.5 % of the scalar baseline at N=1 and under 1 % by N=50. It does not move a crossing that falls above N≈20, but it does put a real 1/N ramp into the small rungs of a curve that is supposed to be flat, so a control statistic over the rungs must exclude them rather than flag them every run.
- **Read a saturation point off the absolute quantity and a crossing off the ratio; they are not interchangeable, and they do not survive a noisy machine equally well.** "Does a bigger batch still buy anything" is a property of the batched side alone, and taking it from the speed-up ratio mixes in the scalar baseline's noise — measured: between N=1,000 and N=2,000 the ratio moved 2.9 % while the batched per-point time moved 0.2 %, all of it the scalar loop. A crossing is the opposite: it is a comparison, so it comes from the within-rung ratio, where drift over the run cancels. **What the absolute quantity costs is reproducibility, so gate the claim on the control rather than publishing it unconditionally.** Three repeat runs of one sweep put saturation at N=1,000, at N=500 and out of reach as the control spread went from 5 % to 22 %, while the crossing held at N=20-50 in all six. A report that prints an absolute-quantity answer from a run whose control failed is publishing the noise; withhold it and say the control failed. More rounds do not fix this - drift is not what `min` over rounds removes.
- **Charts for new report pages are drawn with seaborn, by the renderer, from the stored JSON** (`scripts/batch_break_even_chart.py`, the `benchmark` dependency group). The group is deliberately outside `test` and outside `$(BENCHMARK_ENV)` so no measured process ever imports the plotting stack. matplotlib's SVG writer is not deterministic by default and a committed chart that churns on every render hides the numbers that moved: set `svg.hashsalt` (ids are otherwise salted from `uuid4`), `svg.fonttype="none"` (glyphs are otherwise machine-dependent path outlines) and `savefig(metadata={"Date": None})`, and pin a matplotlib *ceiling* as well as a floor. `scripts/measure_tzfpy_agreement.py`'s hand-written SVG predates this and stays as it is; it is pinned byte for byte by its test.
- **A pair that would have to cross a process is not a pair — measure each pair in one process and refuse the ratio that cannot be.** Three of the harness's four properties are properties of one loop holding both candidates: alternating order, one shared draw, and a round-level win count. Across processes only the best-round ratio survives, which is the estimator on its own that the harness exists to prevent. `make acceleration-paths` is the shape to copy: Numba and pure Python are one source decorated or not, so no process holds both, and each is therefore compared *in its own environment* against the C extension both environments hold. The Numba-against-pure-Python ratio is simply not computed. The two runs' shared C-extension baseline is published beside the tables as the reader's comparability check rather than used as a gate.
- **Never state which acceleration path is fastest anywhere but the generated page.** `utils.py` *prefers* Numba, and until 2026-09 the README, the getting-started page, `7_performance.rst` and `architecture.rst` all read that dispatch rule as a speed claim and repeated a figure for it — one of which (`~400x` for pure Python) no longer matched anything measurable. The ranking moves with the kernels and the data format and is not even one answer across workloads, since a query the shortcut index answers reaches no kernel at all. So: `docs/benchmark_results_acceleration_paths.rst` states it, `make acceleration-paths` regenerates it, and prose elsewhere points there instead of quoting it. The same applies to comments in `scripts/assert_acceleration_path.py`, which used to carry a ratio of their own.
- **Installing Numba changes more than the point-in-polygon kernel.** `validate_coordinates` then calls two `njit`'d scalar helpers rather than two plain comparisons, and every query pays it before any geometry. So a clang measurement taken in a Numba environment is not the configuration a plain install runs — which is why the acceleration-path page publishes both runs' shared clang baseline instead of dividing one run into the other.
